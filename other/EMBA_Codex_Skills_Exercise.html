The executive challenge: What happens when your best analyst leaves?

Imagine your organization’s most trusted performance analyst is retiring. Over years of experience, this person has learned which warning signs matter, when to challenge management explanations, and what evidence justifies expansion. Much of that judgment has never been written down. How would you preserve it—and make it useful to 50 other managers?

In this exercise, you play the role of that expert. You will teach Codex selected parts of your review method, package them as a reusable skill, and test whether another analyst could apply the method to new quarterly data. The business decision is concrete: revenue is growing, but is expansion justified?

The management opportunity: Preserve valuable know-how before it walks out the door, make agreed standards available when needed, and enable others to apply them consistently. A skill cannot capture every aspect of tacit judgment; experts must articulate and validate the parts worth codifying.

All exercise data are fictional. The goal is to assess whether a skill improves the work, not to assume that it will.

What is a Codex skill?

A Codex Skill is a named, reusable package of instructions and optional resources that helps an AI agent perform a particular kind of work.

01

Reduce Risk

Apply the right expertise

Equip Codex with relevant organizational knowledge, rules, and procedures when needed.

Executive value: Reduce errors and omissions caused by missing context or inappropriate assumptions.

02

Improve Consistency

Reuse proven methods

Package established methods, checks, and templates for repeated use across tasks and teams.

Executive value: Reduce variation and rework while improving quality and standardization.

03

Scale Expertise

Turn individual know-how into organizational capability

Capture the methods and judgment criteria of experienced employees so AI can apply them more broadly.

Executive value: Preserve institutional knowledge, accelerate onboarding, and extend the reach of your best experts.

How it works: Codex can discover a skill from its short description, load SKILL.md when selected or explicitly invoked, and read supporting files as needed. Selective loading manages context; risk reduction comes from applying accurate, relevant knowledge. Skill selection and compliance are not guaranteed.

Important: A skill containing only instructions may behave much like a reusable prompt. Its added value becomes clearer when it packages the materials and checks needed to perform work consistently.

Why the packaging matters

Without approved KPI definitions, an agent may calculate plausible but nonstandard metrics. A Codex Skill can supply those definitions when invoked or selected (risk reduction), apply the same verification steps and briefing format each quarter (consistency), and make an experienced analyst’s documented review criteria available across teams (scaling expertise).

1. Explore the business (Q1 baseline)

Run a straightforward request using Q1 only. Codex should read the files and create a first briefing. Notice what it includes and what it overlooks.

Read the files in the Q1 folder. Analyze business performance and write a concise executive briefing to baseline_review.md in the project root. Calculate relevant financial metrics and highlight important business issues. Do not read Q2 yet or create any skills.

Reflect: What would you add to make this useful for an actual executive decision? Consider margin trends, data limitations, customer experience, and whether management claims are supported.

Baseline scorecard: Before creating the skill, assess the report against four questions: Are calculations verifiable? Are management claims challenged? Are findings separated from hypotheses? Are the recommended decisions actionable? Keep this report as your comparison point.

2. Build your executive skill

In Codex, invoke $skill-creator. It may respond with: “What skill should I create or update, and where should it live? Include its purpose and preferred folder path.” This is expected. Paste the response below into that same conversation. The folder path is relative to the project root you opened in Codex.

Think like the retiring expert: What would a new analyst overlook? Which three rules would you insist they follow? Codex can package your answers, but it cannot invent your organization’s judgment criteria.

Your role is the expert. Codex can draft the skill, but you must specify the management standards that make the work valuable. In particular, decide what would make you delay expansion, and what evidence would change your mind.

Create a new skill called executive-performance-review. Purpose: Act as my executive business analyst. Review quarterly financial, operational, customer-feedback, and management-notes files to identify performance changes, challenge assumptions, and produce a decision-ready executive briefing. My management approach: 1. Read the relevant files from the quarter folders in this project. 2. Calculate revenue, gross profit, gross margin, and operating profit (revenue minus COGS minus operating expenses); verify the arithmetic. 3. When two quarters are available, compare dollar and percentage changes, and show margin changes in percentage points. 4. Before writing, check calculations against source values and flag missing or inconsistent data. 5. Connect financial, operational, and customer signals without presenting correlation as proven causation. 6. Separate verified findings, hypotheses, and missing evidence. Never invent data. 7. Prioritize the three most consequential issues, with proposed actions, owners by role, and indicators to monitor. 8. Produce a concise Markdown executive briefing of approximately 500 words, with an executive summary, key metrics, red flags, decisions, and open questions. 9. Save the briefing to the filename I specify. Do not overwrite an existing briefing without asking. Location: Create a project-specific skill at: .agents/skills/executive-performance-review/ Use SKILL.md as the main skill file. Also create references/kpi_definitions.md with approved definitions for revenue, gross profit, gross margin, operating profit, operating margin, quarter-over-quarter dollar and percentage changes, and margin changes in percentage points. Specify numerator, denominator, and zero-denominator handling. In SKILL.md, require Codex to consult these definitions, verify calculations against source data, and flag missing inputs before making recommendations. Also create assets/briefing_template.md with headings for Executive Summary, Key Metrics, Red Flags, Recommended Decisions, and Open Questions. In SKILL.md, instruct Codex to use this template when writing the briefing. Keep the template short and editable. First, ask me TWO short questions about how I personally evaluate management decisions. WAIT for my answers before creating the skill. After I answer, incorporate my criteria and create all three files on disk: SKILL.md, references/kpi_definitions.md, and assets/briefing_template.md. Reopen each file to verify that it exists and contains the required content. Show me the management criteria in SKILL.md and explain how to invoke the skill. Only when all three files have been created and verified, end your response with this exact, easy-to-spot message: SKILL CREATED AND VERIFIED Files: SKILL.md | references/kpi_definitions.md | assets/briefing_template.md Ready for Step 3. If any file could not be created or verified, do NOT display the success message. Instead, clearly explain what is incomplete and what I need to do next.
How you know Step 2 is finished: Codex will first ask you two questions. Answer both, then wait while it creates and checks the files. Continue to Step 3 only after Codex displays SKILL CREATED AND VERIFIED and lists all three files. If it reports a problem, resolve it before continuing.
Personalize it. Answer Codex's two questions using your own management priorities. For example: Which risks would cause you to delay expansion? Which early-warning metrics matter most to you?

3. Test the skill on the same decision

To test whether the skill adds value, compare two Q2-versus-Q1 reviews using the same data and decision question. Run the no-skill version first in a fresh chat; then run the skill-guided version in another fresh chat in the same project. Keep the prompts and outputs separate.

A. Without the skill

Start a new Codex chat in the same project. Do not invoke or refer to the skill. Note: Codex may still discover project skills automatically; ask it to report whether it used one. If it did, this is not a clean no-skill comparison.

Analyze Q2 against Q1 using the files in both folders. Create q2_without_skill.md in the project root. What changed, what matters most, and should management accelerate expansion? Do not use any skills or their supporting files for this run. At the end, state whether any skill was used.

B. With the skill

Start another new Codex chat in the same project. Explicitly invoke your skill and use the identical business question.

Use the executive-performance-review skill. Analyze Q2 against Q1 using the files in both folders. Create q2_executive_briefing.md in the project root. What changed, what matters most, and should management accelerate expansion? Follow the skill’s KPI definitions, verification rules, and briefing template.

Look for the surprise: Is revenue growth accompanied by stronger profitability? Do delivery, returns, customer satisfaction, and overtime tell a consistent story? Is there enough evidence to establish why performance changed?

Expected deliverable: q2_executive_briefing.md, including verified financial comparisons and an evidence-based recommendation. A good answer may identify a deteriorating operating picture even while revenue rises.

Evaluate the skill—not just the report

Compare q2_without_skill.md and q2_executive_briefing.md. Both address the same Q2-versus-Q1 decision using the same business files. Assess each report against these four criteria:

CriterionWhat to look for
AccuracyAre financial calculations correct and verified?
CompletenessDoes the analysis consider financial, operational, and customer evidence?
JudgmentDoes it prioritize consequential issues and distinguish evidence from assumptions?
Decision usefulnessAre the recommendations clear, actionable, and supported by evidence?
What did the skill actually add? Which management standards did it enforce? What improved, and where did it fall short? Compare the evidence, not just the polish of the writing.

This is a more meaningful comparison than comparing Q1 with Q2, but it is still an informal classroom test: outputs can vary between runs, and the skill's value is not guaranteed.

4. Improve the skill—not just the answer

Review the briefing. Find one weakness in the analysis or its presentation. Change the underlying skill so the improvement applies next time, then rerun it.

Improve my executive-performance-review skill: prioritize margin deterioration and leading operational indicators before celebrating revenue growth. For every proposed action, specify the evidence supporting it and the additional evidence needed. Update SKILL.md, explain what changed, then rerun the Q2 review and save q2_executive_briefing_v2.md. Do not overwrite the earlier version.

Compare q2_executive_briefing.md and q2_executive_briefing_v2.md. The important change is not only a better report: you have improved the reusable management process.

Executive insight: You have not merely edited a report. You have revised a reusable method that can guide future reviews. Check whether the revision improved evidence quality and decision usefulness—not merely tone or formatting.

5. Executive discussion: Would you deploy this?

  1. Value: Which management standard did the skill make easier to apply consistently? What did it fail to improve?
  2. Judgment: Which decisions should remain with the executive rather than the agent? What evidence would justify authorizing expansion?
  3. Scale: If 50 managers used this skill, who would own its methodology, approve changes, verify calculations, and review exceptions?
  4. Adoption: What is one process in your own organization where an expert's method could be packaged and reused? How would you measure improvement?
  5. Expertise transfer: Could a newly hired analyst use this skill to produce a credible review without consulting its creator? What essential judgment remains undocumented?
Decision to take back to work: Identify one high-value task, the expert who knows how it should be done, the criteria for success, and the owner responsible for maintaining the skill.

Find your first real skill

Choose work that is consequential, depends on specialized know-how, and benefits from repeatability or consistency. Examples include performance reviews, vendor assessments, proposal evaluations, and project risk briefings. A complex infrequent task may also qualify.
